## OAK CITY SETTLEMENT — PART 2: LAND EXPANSION THROUGH THE SENDOFF

She pointed at a corner lot about a block and a half off the main plaza. There was a shed on it—two rooms, or the structural suggestion of two rooms, the kind of building that had survived this long through stubbornness rather than quality. The siding had gone silver-gray with age. The roof had a sag in the middle that suggested it had been losing a slow argument with gravity for years. A scrap of wire mesh was doing the work the fence used to do.

The lot was small. It was the kind of small that had just enough room for the building and not much else—the footprint of a city that had been adding density for decades without stopping to ask whether what it was building was any good.

Soliera looked at it.

Then She raised Her hand.

M85 — THE LAND EXPANDS

The first thing that happened was the land.

Not the shed—the land. The corner lot simply... extended. There was no mechanism for it, no seam, no visible physics that explained how an acre of level, good-soiled, walled ground appeared around a city-block corner lot that had no business containing it. The neighboring buildings didn't move. The road didn't shift. But the lot expanded outward with the quiet certainty of something that had always been that size and the city had simply been confused.

Grass followed the expansion, short and even, still catching the morning light, with no evidence that it had been anything else. A stone path appeared from the front gate to where the door used to be, smooth and dry.

And then the shed.

No one who watched it could agree afterward on the exact sequence. The structure bent. Contracted. The walls folded into each other and out again. The roof dropped and rose. It wasn't a collapse and it wasn't an explosion—it was more like watching clay respond to invisible hands that knew exactly what they were making. The single-story two-room structure spread. Gained height. Gained proportion. Gained, somewhere in the middle of the process, the particular character of a building that had been designed to be beautiful and had succeeded.

M86 — THE HOUSE STANDS

When it was done, a three-story colonial home stood on a quiet acre in the middle of Oak City, white and gold from the porch columns to the uppermost window trim, its paint perfect, its lines clean, its porch wide enough for a family to sit on. The trellis beside the front door already had something green growing up it. The chimney stack had not been there before and was there now, settled into the roofline as if the bricklayer had finished it decades ago.

The crowd watching from the street went quiet in the particular way crowds go quiet when they have run out of referents.

This was not, strictly speaking, the first impossible thing they had seen in the last several days. It was perhaps the fifteenth. But there was something about the house—the specific, domestic, completely unnecessary beauty of it—that landed differently than walls being deleted or bullets being caught. Those things were power. This was preference. This was gods deciding what they wanted to live in and making it.

An old man near the back of the crowd took his hat off.

Inside, Ember squealed.

The sound curtain dropped as they crossed the threshold—not a silence, but an addition: the distant, impossible sound of crickets and birds in a city that had neither, wrapped around the interior like a second skin. The main room was oval, with the red silk couch taking up the interior wall, and the food bar running the right side fully stocked with things that hadn't been in the city an hour ago. To the left, mirrors and closets and dressers occupied a dressing corner that Sera walked into immediately and began evaluating with professional focus.

The rear held storage. Above it all, a spiral stair wound up to a loft with one-way glass that looked out over the front acre without being visible from below.

Sera came back out of the dressing corner with three opinions and no hesitation.

Niv stood in the doorway, surveying the interior with his hands behind his back, head tilted at a fractional angle.

"Good bones," he said.

Veyren moved without being asked to the corner nearest the door—the natural post for someone whose job was to be between anything coming in and everything that mattered—and settled there with the specific stillness of a man who has found his spot.

Bucky produced a small tag from somewhere in his coat, marked it with two words in his own shorthand—evidence / domestic—and pressed it to the door frame just above eye level. Sanctum domestic. Invite only. Anyone with the training to read Sanctum markings would know exactly what that meant.

Anyone without the training would simply notice that the door was never quite as inviting as it should have been, and feel no strong desire to knock uninvited.


M87–M89: CHURCH SIDEBAR / PROCESSION / NEW NORMAL

M87 — CHURCH SIDEBAR

Malachar sat in the back of the Church SUV with his hands folded in his lap and looked at nothing in particular for a long time.

Ysbet's notebook was open. She wrote several lines, crossed one out, wrote it again differently.

It was Hale who finally said something.

"I always thought—" he started, and then stopped, because the sentence had too many possible ends and he wasn't sure which one was true. He looked out the tinted window at the plaza, at the titan's unmoving form, at the house that had not existed this morning.

"I knew something was real," he said instead, more carefully. "I've spent thirty years in the Church. I prayed. I kept the rituals. I did all the correct things, and I believed, the way you believe something that you've decided to believe because the alternative is a world with no floor."

Malachar didn't respond.

"And now," Hale said, "I've seen a woman catch a fifty-caliber round and ask what it was. I've watched a god raise Her hand and move an acre of land. I've stood in the shadow of something that makes the Church's architecture look like it was drawn in crayon." He paused. "And I'm not afraid. I thought I would be afraid. I thought if it was ever actually real, I would be afraid."

"What are you?" Ysbet asked, not unkindly.

Hale considered it. "Vindicated," he said.

Malachar made a small sound—not dismissive, not agreeing. A sound like pressure releasing from somewhere he hadn't known was pressurized. He looked at his hands.

"The Church's jurisdiction," he said, very quietly, "has always been spiritual authority. The right to define what is sacred, what is not. What is permitted, what is not." He was quiet for a moment. "We've spent centuries using that as a wall."

He didn't finish the thought. He didn't need to.

Ysbet finished a line in her notebook. "I'm going to request a liaison assignment with the Oak City Company officials," she said. "We should have a formal presence here. A positive one. The Church doesn't need to be in opposition to any of this. It never needed to be."

Malachar nodded, once, slowly.

M88 — PROCESSION

The convoy completed its movement through the city in the afternoon, knights palms-out, vehicles at walking pace, Company holding the side lane. No horns. No PA. No announcements. The city watched through storefronts and from high windows, and the thing it watched was: orderly, unhurried, and not threatening. The Church coming through like water finding its new level.

M89 — NEW NORMAL

By the third day, it had stopped being a spectacle.

Sanctum stewards and Oak City locals had developed the same rhythm that forms naturally when two groups of people discover they have more in common than expected and the work gives them something to stand on together. Fields were being surveyed. Roads were being mapped. Three Sanctum cooks had established something like a permanent presence at the central market and the line for their food was, by all accounts, extraordinary.

The Company logged it all: integration proceeding. Infrastructure improving. No friction. The city was getting better. It was happening so naturally that the strangest thing about it was how unsurprising it felt.

The titan did not move.

It simply stood in the plaza, patient as weather.

M90: THE SPAR / THE SENDOFF

Nobody said, out loud, that the girls were leaving soon. It was the kind of knowledge that lived in the edges—in the way Soliera had taken to standing at the city's northern boundary for longer stretches, looking out at the highway spine and the distant shimmer of neon on the horizon. In the way Ember kept finding new things to say a proper first goodbye to: the pancake vendor, the old man who always sat on the bench near the plaza gate, a cat that had started following her around two days ago.

The spar started, as most of Sera's ideas did, without a formal announcement.

M90 — THE SPAR

She appeared in the cleared lot behind the command post and looked at Cassian.

"Fight me," she said.

Cassian looked at her. He looked at Veyren. He looked at Niv, who had appeared at the doorway of the command post building with a cup of tea and the expression of a man who is watching a weather event he has studied extensively and remains, on balance, delighted by.

"Play fight you," Cassian said.

"Yes," Sera agreed. "Obviously."

It lasted the better part of an hour. This was not because Cassian or Veyren or Niv were ever close to winning—the concept of winning, in the way it would normally apply, was simply not in operation. What was in operation was Sera enjoying herself. She moved in wide, unserious arcs. She let exchanges develop. She telegraphed. She gave Cassian three whole seconds to get a grip on her arm and then shrugged him off like a coat that had slipped and did it with a grin and the words "good grip, actually."

Niv entered the rotation near the forty-minute mark with the air of a professor confirming a hypothesis he had already documented in extensive notes. The first exchange was brief. The second was briefer. The third, he didn't bother with—he held up a hand, Sera stopped, and he nodded once with the particular satisfaction of a man who has just confirmed that his models were correct.

Ember watched from the fence with Amara, eating something wrapped in paper, providing commentary that was mostly about how fast Sera was moving and not at all about strategy, which was fine because Amara was providing the strategy commentary, and neither of them could meaningfully influence the outcome regardless.

Soliera stood at the edge of the lot and watched all three of them throw their best at Sera with a quiet, settled expression on Her face.

M90.1 — THE SENDOFF

It was late afternoon when the girls left.

No speech. No ceremony. No formal goodbye. Bucky was on the command post steps when they came past. He gave a half-nod—the kind that means: I see you, I know what this is, you've been good company—and Sera gave him a wave that was more of a salute, loose-wristed and bright-eyed, and that was most of it.

Ember turned back twice. Once to look at the house—the white and gold colonial standing quiet on its impossible acre, porch empty, trellis already beginning to settle into its new permanence. Once more to find the cat.

The cat, for its part, had disappeared. Ember looked for it for a moment, then seemed to decide it was fine wherever it was.

They walked out of Oak City on the north road as the light went orange, four figures moving without particular hurry toward the highway spine and the distant, hard neon crown of the city that had spent decades learning how to keep things out.

Behind them, Oak City breathed in the new shape they had left it.

The titan stood in the plaza and said nothing and did not move.

It never moved.
