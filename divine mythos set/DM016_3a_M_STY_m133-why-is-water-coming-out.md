# M133 — WHY IS WATER COMING OUT

"He's happier!" she said, delighted. "Amara, why is water coming out?"

"Because he is," Amara said simply.

Ember considered this, accepted it, and turned back to him with an expression of uncomplicated satisfaction.

Amara, standing at Ember's shoulder, watched him for another moment.

"He's a gentleman," she said, quietly, the way she said it about anyone who counted — which was everyone. The first time she'd said it, it had been to explain him to Ember. This time it was for him.

He heard it.

It landed on him the way small things sometimes land harder than large things: sideways, unexpectedly, in the part of the chest that had not been prepared for it. He'd been healed. His body had been returned to him. Those were enormous things, impossible things, and he was still processing them at the edge of his ability. But to be called that — called that word, by someone who clearly meant it the way it was supposed to be meant, without adjustment, without qualification, without the particular hesitation that usually preceded any word applied to him — that was something different.

He was still figuring out what these beings were. He was beginning to understand that whatever they were, they were not here to take anything. He had been in the presence of powerful people before. Powerful people, in his experience, always needed something from the space around them: attention, fear, deference, resources. These ones seemed to be simply walking through his alley the way wind walked through his alley — present, moving, transforming the air, not asking for anything in return.

He bowed his head. Held his arm out a second time so Ember could touch it again.

"Yay!!" Ember said, and patted it with both hands, thoroughly satisfied.

# M134 — THE FAMILY AT THE END OF THE BLOCK

The family was at the end of the alley block.

A woman standing in a doorway with an infant held close to her chest, both arms wrapped around him the way women hold children when they're not sure what's coming — not tightly enough to hurt, tightly enough to run. The infant was small in the wrong way: too light, too quiet, the specific quietness of a child whose lungs had been working too hard for too long to have much left over for sound.

Beside her, a man had stepped out from the interior. He was thin — the kind of thin that meant not enough, that meant for a while now — but he had put himself between his family and the alley with the full commitment of someone who had decided that this was the position and he was holding it regardless of what that meant. He didn't have anything in his hands. He didn't need to; the stance was the statement.

Sera looked at him and her whole expression softened in a way it didn't soften very often — not into sympathy exactly, but into the particular warmth she had for people doing the right thing with what they had.

"Helloooo!" she said, bright and easy, raising a hand. "I'm Sera. Over there is Ember." She gestured. "And behind me is God."

The woman's grip on the infant tightened slightly. The man didn't move.

From behind Sera, Soliera stepped into the edge of the alley's light — not forward, not aggressively, just: present, the way She was always present, which was completely. The man looked at Her. Whatever argument he had been ready to make with his body rearranged itself into something else, something he didn't have the architecture to express.

He stayed where he was. He didn't step aside. But his shoulders — very slightly, almost imperceptibly — came down.

The infant coughed.

It was a wet sound. The kind that had been going on for a while and had settled into the family like weather settles into an old building: not acute, not sudden, just always present, wearing at everything beneath it. The woman's face contracted around it in the small, practiced way of people who have stopped reacting fully to something because reacting fully every time would cost too much.

Sera stopped.

Not because she'd been moving fast — she hadn't been. But her forward motion ceased completely, the way a river stops being a river when it hits a wall, and all her attention redirected.

She looked at the infant. She looked at the mother. She looked at the father.


M137 — I NORMALLY DON'T EVEN ASK

"You want us to help him?" she asked. Her voice had gone level, simple. "I normally don't even ask."

She almost said the last part to herself — nearly wondered aloud why she'd bothered, because the answer was of course, of course, it was always going to be of course — but something in her had decided to say it out loud anyway. Soliera gave people the decision. Soliera moved through the world in a way that left people's agency intact even when She was rewriting what their agency meant. Sera was learning that. It didn't come naturally. She was learning it anyway.

The mother's face broke.

Not dramatically. Not with a sound. Just: the specific collapse of a person who has been holding something up for a very long time and has just been told, for the first time, that they are allowed to put it down.

She nodded. One small, fierce nod.

The father's jaw worked. He looked at Soliera, then at Sera, then at his wife, then back at Soliera. He gave a short, sharp nod that meant the same thing.


M140 — THE INFANT IS HEALED / CASCADE BEGINS

Soliera stepped close.

One hand, raised — the same motion She made when She was reaching for something just outside the frame of what was visible. Not a performance. Not a ceremony. The gesture of someone doing a small, obvious thing that had simply been waiting to be done.

The infant's back straightened. His lungs — which had been working around scar tissue, around fluid, around the long architecture of untreated illness — cleared. The jaundice went with it. His weight returned: not dramatically, not all at once, but enough that the mother's arms registered the change and her grip tightened instinctively, recalibrating.

She looked down at him.

He looked up at her with the clear, uncomplicated attention of a child whose body had just stopped hurting and was interested in what the world looked like from here.

The mother made a sound that was not a word.

She nearly dropped him — not from shock, exactly, but because the sudden, correct weight of a healthy child after months of the wrong weight is a physical fact that the arms need a moment to adjust to. She caught him. She held him. Her face did several things very fast and then settled into something that had no name in any language except maybe the very old ones that were mostly not spoken anymore.

The father's legs gave out.

He didn't fall — he lowered himself, slowly, deliberately, like a man choosing to kneel rather than being forced to. His daughter — a small girl who had been pressed to the wall behind him through all of this — leaped at him the moment he was low enough to reach, wrapping both arms around his neck with the utter unselfconsciousness of a child who does not yet understand that adults sometimes need a moment.

He held her. His eyes were closed.

Tehilum, who had followed the group a cautious two steps behind and had watched all of this unfold with the expression of a man revising his entire understanding of what a day could contain, made a sound that was something between a laugh and an exhale and pressed the back of his hand to his mouth.

Ember was already waving at everyone.

"Hi!!" she called out to the alley at large, turning in a slow circle, addressing the faces on the balconies and in the doorways and at the ends of the cross-alleys who had been watching in absolute silence for the last several minutes. "Hi! Hi!"

Sera planted her hands on her hips, chin up, and surveyed the gathered audience with the expression of a queen reviewing new subjects — not unkind, not distant, just: taking stock, finding it acceptable, approving.

Soliera stood in the center of the alley and watched all of it with the same serene stillness She brought to everything: the kind of stillness that doesn't mean nothing is happening. The kind that means something is happening at a level so fundamental that the surface has gone very quiet.

# M141–M145: RISA'S HOME / THE FEAST

It started the way these things always started — one person moving toward something good, and then two people, and then everyone, and then the question of where they were all moving became a practical problem that needed solving.

Risa solved it.

# M142 — RISA'S APARTMENT

She lived — had lived, once — three floors up in Alley 14, Grid 7, in a building that had made it this far through community stubbornness and the collective refusal of its residents to acknowledge structural pessimism. The staircase was fine if you didn't think about it too hard. The apartment was two rooms with a window that faced the alley and a roof leak that moved around seasonally, as if it had opinions about where it wanted to be.

She led them there without fully deciding to. It was simply the place she knew, the place her feet knew, and the alternative was standing in the alley while the crowd grew until the alley became impassable, which it was going to do very shortly regardless.


M143 — SANCTUM QUALITY

The apartment was not Sanctum-quality.

It was Sanctum-quality for approximately ninety seconds after they crossed the threshold.

The cabinets filled without anyone opening them — the soft, settled weight of full shelves registering through the wood and the hinges as they took on a new purpose. The water from the tap ran clear, and then ran cleaner than it had run in years, and then ran the way water was supposed to run when it had been treated as something worth maintaining. The roof leak closed. The window frame stopped rattling in its casement.

In the alley below, something was happening to the electrical poles.

They had been leaning for so long that the lean had become their default state, their natural geometry, the way a person carries an old injury until they've forgotten what upright felt like. They straightened now — not violently, not with any sound — just: finding the vertical, returning to the position they'd been built for. The wiring above them went taut in the clean, purposeful way of infrastructure that has been maintained. The hum that had been in the walls and the wiring and the background noise of Grid 7 for as long as anyone could remember — the constant, low-frequency anxiety of systems running past capacity — went quiet.

People in the surrounding apartments looked up from whatever they were doing.

Risa stood in the middle of her apartment, which was her apartment but also now somehow not the same apartment at all, and looked at the cabinets, and looked at the window, and looked at Ember, who was examining a small decoration on the wall with two fingers and an expression of scholarly interest.

"Risa," Amara said, from behind her.

"Yes," Risa said.

"Are you alright?"

"I'm," Risa started, and then didn't finish, because she was doing the arithmetic of everything that had happened today and the sum kept coming out as a number that didn't have a name yet.

The feast assembled itself the way the Sanctum's presence always assembled things — not from nothing, but from abundance meeting need, from the particular organizational intelligence of five thousand people who had been doing exactly this in city after city and knew how it was supposed to go. Tables appeared in the alley below. Food appeared on the tables: real food, not the compressed and rationed kind, not the charity kind, but the kind that existed in a world that had decided nourishment was worth doing properly.

Word had been moving through Grid 7 for the last twenty minutes, faster than anyone had walked, and the alley filled.

# M145 — THE CONVOY ARRIVES

The convoy arrived with the evening.

It came through the breach in the wall the way the Sanctum's presence always arrived — in full, without apology, with the complete organizational confidence of something that had never once had to wonder whether it was welcome. A mile of constructs, stewards, supply lines, civic crews, and everything that came with them: tools, food, medicine, the particular expertise of people who treated urban improvement as a straightforward problem with known solutions.

The city's corporate monitoring systems logged its arrival. Several analysts sent several reports to several administrators. Executive F's office received its copy and he read it with the expression of a man confirming a thing he had already understood was going to happen.

In Grid 7, the convoy's advance teams spread out through the alley grid with the easy efficiency of people who have done this in Oak City and seventeen places before Oak City, and who have learned that the work goes better when you ask first and then do, rather than the other way around. They asked. They were told. They began.

The feast was already going.

Tehilum was at a table near the building's front wall, sitting in a circle of people who had never particularly talked to him before today and were now talking to him, because today had reshuffled the social geometry of the alley in ways that would take time to fully understand but were already making themselves felt. He had food in front of him. He kept touching his jaw, absently, in the specific way of someone checking on a thing that used to hurt and finding, with the fresh surprise each time, that it didn't anymore.

Ember was in the middle of everything, as Ember was always in the middle of everything: moving between tables, touching things, asking questions, receiving answers with the bright, focused attention of someone for whom the world is continuously, genuinely interesting. She had found a child roughly her own height and they had spent several minutes in what appeared to be a serious discussion about the structural properties of a wooden crate.

Sera had taken a position on a broad windowsill above the alley, legs hanging over the edge, watching the whole thing with the particular satisfaction of someone who has organized an outcome and is now enjoying seeing it run. She had food. She was eating it. She was watching her Ember in the crowd below with the warm, proprietary attention of an older sister watching a younger one discover a new place.

Soliera sat on the building's outer steps, unhurried, in the quiet at the edge of the noise. People gave Her a radius without being asked to — not out of fear, not exactly out of awe, but out of the instinctive sense that She was doing something important that required the space, which was: being at peace, in a place that had not had much peace in it recently, and letting that fact exist.

The evening settled around them like it had always planned to be there.
