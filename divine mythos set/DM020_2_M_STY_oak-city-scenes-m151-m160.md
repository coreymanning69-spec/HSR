## SERA'S PLATE TEST (Morning Session — Full Prose)

Sera stood under the plate and looked up through her half-cupped fingers and every line of her was a thing the room couldn't stop looking at.

Not flat-palmed. Not spread wide the way Veyren had spread his hands against the grip ridges, filling the contact points with four feet of arm and three hundred and eighty pounds of intent. Her hand was half-cupped — fingers curved upward, the natural resting position of a hand that wasn't bracing for anything, the shape a hand makes when it's holding something invisible or reaching for something it expects to receive. Casual. The geometry of someone who hadn't adjusted for the task because the task didn't require adjustment.

She looked like — the cadets would struggle with this later, each of them reaching for a different comparison and none of them adequate — she looked like someone waiting for the bass to drop. Like a performer in the wings. Like the lead image on an album cover or a magazine spread, the composition so precise it seemed staged, the bare shoulders and the almost-red silk and the collarbone catching the flat fluorescent light and turning it into something that belonged in a different building. An idol. A k-pop star frozen mid-concept, every angle intentional, every surface flawless, the physical reality of her so aggressively beautiful that it looped past attraction into something closer to vertigo.

"Alright," she said. Looking up at the plate. The grin was there — not wide, not sharp, just present, the resting expression of a woman who was about to have a good time. "Well, come on. Let's get this going." She shifted her weight from one bare foot to the other. "I wanna see how strong this thing is."

Not how strong *she* was. How strong the *thing* was.

Contact.

The display flickered. Twelve pounds.

Her fingertips didn't move. The half-cupped hand received the plate's initial contact the way a table receives a coffee cup — without acknowledgment, without adjustment, without any visible registration that a new force was being applied to it. Twelve pounds across five fingertips. Two and a half pounds per finger. The weight of a bag of flour distributed across a hand that could have been holding a glass of wine at a party for all the structural response it showed.

Fifty pounds.

Her fingertips didn't move.

One hundred.

Two hundred.

The display climbed in amber increments and Sera stood beneath the plate with one arm raised and the other at her side and her bare feet flat on the cracked concrete and her expression unchanged and the room watched the number grow the way you watch a countdown except the countdown was going up and nobody knew where it ended and the woman under it was looking at her own fingertips with mild interest, the way someone checks their nails while waiting for a bus.

There was a smell.

Faint at first — metallic, sharp, the particular tang of heated steel that anyone who'd worked in a machine shop or stood near a grinding wheel would recognize. The contact points. Five fingertips pressing into the underside of a twelve-thousand-pound steel plate, and the plate was pressing back with increasing hydraulic force, and the interface between Sera's fingers and the plate's surface was generating friction heat. Not because her fingers were sliding. Because the plate was trying to compress past them and couldn't, and the molecular interface between her skin and the steel was converting the failed compression into thermal energy, and the thermal energy had nowhere to go because her fingers weren't conducting it away. They weren't conducting anything. They were just *there*, half-cupped, curved, immovable in the specific way that things are immovable when the concept of movement doesn't apply to them.

Five hundred pounds. A thousand.

The smell grew.

Two thousand pounds. The number Veyren had passed through on his way to fifteen six, the number that had made the cadets murmur, the number that corresponded to the weight of a compact car. Sera's hand was perfectly level. The half-cup hadn't flattened. The fingers hadn't spread or straightened or adjusted in any way. The plate was pressing down with a ton of hydraulic force and the hand beneath it existed in exactly the same configuration it had existed in before the machine started, and the plate was the thing that was changing.

Not yet visibly. Not yet structurally. But the smell was telling the story before the eyes could. The friction heat was climbing. The steel at the contact points — the five small areas where Sera's fingertips met the plate's underside — was warming past ambient, past body temperature, past the threshold where the human nose detects heated metal. The engineer's instruments would later show localized temperatures at those five points exceeding two hundred degrees within the first sixty seconds. The plate was not designed to experience point-source heating from below. The plate was designed to press downward against things that gave way. It was encountering something that didn't give way, and the encounter was producing heat, and the heat was a love letter from physics to the room explaining in olfactory terms what was about to happen.

Five thousand.

Eight thousand.

Ten thousand.

Sera yawned.

She brought her other hand up — the free one, the one that wasn't holding ten thousand pounds of hydraulic press — and covered her mouth with an exaggerated stretch, the theatrical yawn of someone performing boredom for an audience, the kind of yawn a teenager does in a classroom to communicate to their friends that this is beneath them. Her eyes closed. Her jaw opened wide. She made a small sound — *aahhh* — and let the hand drop back to her side and blinked twice, slowly, like someone waking up from a nap that she'd decided to take in the middle of holding five tons over her head with her fingertips.

Dario made a sound. Not a word — a sound. The vocalization that escapes when the brain encounters something it categorized as impossible and the impossible thing yawns. The sound was somewhere between a laugh and a gasp and it came out of him involuntarily and he clamped his mouth shut immediately afterward but the sound had already happened and three cadets near him had made similar sounds and the room was vibrating with the specific frequency of people who were watching a woman yawn under ten thousand pounds and didn't know what to do with their faces.

Twelve thousand. Thirteen thousand.

Thirteen thousand. The weight that had made them worry about Veyren. The weight that had produced concerned expressions and muttered calculations and the shared unspoken thought that he should be paste. Sera was holding it with half-cupped fingertips and she had just yawned and her feet were —

Her feet.

Sera didn't wear shoes. The bare feet that had walked through the gate plaza and across the café floor and through the testing corridor and over Veyren's starburst cracks were planted on the reinforced concrete pad beneath the press plate, and the concrete was losing.

Veyren was grinning. Of course he was. The enormous, easy, joyful grin of a man who had held fifteen thousand six hundred pounds and understood exactly what it felt like and was watching someone hold twice that with her fingertips while yawning and was feeling not diminished but *proud*. She was his. He was hers. The thing she was doing reflected upward through the possession model and landed on him as validation — not of his achievement, which was real and earned and his own, but of his *position*. He was in the circle of something that could do this. The circle was warm and the woman at its center was four foot ten and her hand was seven inches deep in industrial steel and she was the most beautiful thing he'd ever seen and the beauty was inseparable from the violence was inseparable from the joy was inseparable from the fact that she'd said *good boy* to him twenty minutes ago and meant it.

Ember was bouncing on her toes. Sparks cascading off her fire-hair in copper arcs, her hands clasped in front of her chest, her eyes enormous and bright with the reflected glow of heated steel. She was watching Sera the way she always watched Sera — with the complete, uncomplicated adoration of a child watching her older sister do the thing the older sister does best. No fear. No awe. No existential processing. Just delight. Pure, thermal, sparking delight, because Sera was showing off and showing off was what Sera did and it was always good and it was always fun and the fact that the showing off involved pushing her hand through a steel plate didn't change the emotional category. It was the same as the punch. The same as the wall. The same as every time Sera did something impossible and Ember clapped. The scale changed. The joy didn't.

Cassian watched his display. The stress indicators were flashing. The hydraulic system was at ninety-seven percent. The plate deformation was exceeding the sensor array's ability to measure — several of the sixteen contact sensors had lost signal entirely, their mounting points distorted beyond the tolerance of the wiring. The data stream was degrading, the numbers becoming unreliable, the machine's ability to report what was happening to it failing in real time because what was happening to it was not something it had been designed to report.

The display read thirty thousand and climbing. Thirty-one. Thirty-two. The numbers were slowing because the machine was approaching its mechanical limit, the hydraulics reaching the ceiling of what they could produce, the system running out of force to apply. Not because Sera had reached a limit. Because the *machine* had reached a limit. The press was redlining and the hand was seven inches deep and the plate was deformed and the smell of hot metal was in everyone's lungs and the woman under all of it was standing in concrete that had accepted her feet like a welcome mat and her expression was —

Peaceful.

Not triumphant. Not fierce. Not performing the dominance that the room's emotional vocabulary would have predicted. Peaceful. The calm of someone doing what they were made to do, in front of the person they were made to do it for, and finding the doing sufficient. The theological core of Sera's existence, expressed as a woman standing under a machine with her hand in its heart and her feet in the ground and her eyes soft and her mouth holding the faintest curve of a smile that wasn't for the cadets or the display or the burning metal but for the silent woman standing behind her who was watching and whose watching was the whole point and always had been.

Soliera watched.

She stood where she always stood — behind, silent, present in the way that weather is present, the way gravity is present, the way the ground is present. She watched her daughter's hand move through steel the way she watched everything Sera did — with the complete, unperformative attention of someone for whom watching was sufficient and sufficient was everything. No expression. No reaction. No visible response to the smell or the sound or the display or the cadets' various collapses into tears or trembling or dropped tablets. She watched, and the watching was the purpose, and Sera could feel the watching the way plants feel sunlight — not as information but as sustenance, as the environmental condition that made growth possible, as the thing that being alive was *for*.

The machine groaned. The hydraulics had reached their ceiling. Thirty-four thousand, eight hundred pounds. The display held there, trembling, the amber digits flickering with the uncertainty of a system operating at its absolute maximum output, every component stressed to its rated limit, every seal and gasket and hydraulic line performing at the edge of its design envelope.

Sera's hand was embedded in steel. Her feet were in concrete. The plate was deformed around her fingers like foil around a fist.

Then she lowered her arm.

The plate gave up the shape of her the way water gives up the shape of a hand — not all at once, not with any sound of release, but with a low sustained groan that began in the hydraulics and moved outward through the steel frame and into the concrete floor and into the feet of everyone standing on it, a vibration felt more than heard, the machine exhaling after holding something it was never designed to hold. The depression in the underside of the plate — the exact geometry of five half-cupped fingertips, seven inches deep, the walls of it smooth as cast metal — stayed. The plate kept it. The shape of her hand in industrial steel, permanent, the way footprints stay in wet concrete long after the foot has gone.

The contact points were glowing.

Cherry. Not warm — *cherry*. The five areas where her fingertips had pressed into the plate's underside were lit from within by two hundred degrees of accumulated heat, each one a precise circle of red-orange steel surrounded by its own faint oxide ring, the iridescent bloom of rapidly-cooled metal going through its color states. They pulsed faintly with the dissipating heat. They were beautiful in the way dangerous things are beautiful when the danger has already passed and left only the evidence.

She rolled her neck once and stepped off the pad.

The foot imprints were still there. Both of them. Pressed into reinforced concrete to a depth of three-quarters of an inch at the heel and ball, less at the arch, the exact topography of her bare feet rendered permanently into a surface rated to withstand tank traffic. The concrete hadn't cracked around them — it had simply reorganized, accepting the pressure the way clay accepts a stamp, the molecular structure of the material compressing and settling and deciding, in the way that materials decide things, that this was its new shape. The harder material simply becomes the shape of the softer one, when the softer one is that. The engineers would measure it later and the measurement would not help them.

Veyren stood at the edge of the pad and looked at the prints and did the math the only way he knew how — against his own fifteen thousand six hundred pounds and the starburst of cracking that had radiated from under his feet. The starburst. He had cracked reinforced concrete under pressure. She had left footprints in it the way you leave footprints in sand. The math produced a ratio he did not attempt to name.

Ember crouched at the edge of the near print and extended one finger toward it without touching. The warmth radiating from the contact points above reached her even from there, and she tilted her head and looked at the imprint and then looked up at Sera with the expression of someone who has just seen a magic trick performed by someone who doesn't know they're doing magic. Delight. Pure and uncomplicated and thermal, a small cascade of sparks running off her hair and landing in the concrete dust around the edge of the print.

Cassian's frameworks were not equipped. He had several of them — professional, technical, institutional — and he was cycling through them and none of them were producing outputs that felt adequate. The data from the last two minutes sat in his display and refused to resolve into a briefing. He would write three drafts of the incident report over the following week. He would delete all three.

Dario stepped toward the prints without deciding to. He looked down at them for a long moment. His pen was still uncapped from when he'd stopped writing at twelve thousand pounds and hadn't started again.

Sera walked to where she'd left her things and picked up her water and drank half of it and set it back down, the effect of someone finishing a song — not triumphant, not diminished, just done, the performance complete, the instrument at rest. She glanced once over her shoulder at the still-glowing plate.

"Good machine," she said. To the machine.

Soliera had not moved. She stood where she had stood for the duration, in the same position, with the same expression, watching. As she had watched before the machine started and would have watched regardless of what the machine produced. She was the environmental condition of the room. She was the reason the room existed. Sera could feel the watching the way she always felt it — not as attention but as the condition of being alive at all — and the feeling was sufficient, and sufficient was everything, and the plate test was over.
