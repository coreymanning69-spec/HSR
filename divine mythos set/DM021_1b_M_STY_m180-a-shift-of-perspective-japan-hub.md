### M180 — A Shift of Perspective (Japan Hub)

Sixteen monitoring stations go dark at 7:42.

In the briefing room, the display board registers it sequentially: Hub 1, Hub 4, Hub 7, the remaining thirteen in a cascade that takes less than a second to complete. The signals do not drop, which would indicate equipment failure. They stop, which indicates the thing they were monitoring has stopped requiring monitoring. This is a distinction the Pacific division's monitoring infrastructure has no existing category for. The board sits for two seconds without updating, waiting for a restore signal that does not come. Then the status reclassification completes, and where sixteen markers read ACTIVE and SSS-CLASS they now read CLOSED — a status the board has never been asked to display before. The field does not have an appropriate color for it. It displays it in gray.

Kiyuru is standing at the window when it happens.

He does not have the feed. He has the sensation, which arrives before the data: a specific change in pressure, or resonance — the world's weight redistributing itself by a margin that should not be measurable and is. He has spent enough time in proximity to things older than nations to recognize when something is resolved. What has just been resolved is eleven years and forty-seven dead and a signal transmitted from 312 meters below sea level that a woman died saying the word *beautiful.*

"The world is quieter," he says, "than it was."

Executive J looks up from the board. He does not ask what Kiyuru means, which would be wasting time. He requests the Oak City feed.

The feed loads. Gate Leviathan's closure timestamp: 7:42:11. The Oak City timestamp for the prior morning event — the report from the testing facility, which J has read three times because the data quality in the original draft was too controlled for what it was describing — lands at 7:41:58. Thirteen seconds elapsed between the testing-facility event and the gate closure. J pulls both files. The testing-facility report's summary notation, in Cassian's handwriting, is one word: *condensed.* Which J had read as institutional modesty on first pass, the way a report sometimes describes a result as within acceptable parameters when what it means is that it fell within the parameters it had — not within any parameters the machinery was built for.

There is a margin note on the back page. Same handwriting. *There is no ceiling.*

J looks at the board for a while. The gray CLOSED status on sixteen positions that have held SSS-CLASS for eleven years. The gate that wasn't closeable. The morning report from Oak City that was too quiet about what it was describing.

He picks up the personal line.


### M180.1 — The Call

F answers on the third ring. He is in the morning, on the girls' side of the world. From the sound of it, somewhere domestic.

J says: "The Leviathan reading."

"I know," F says. He does not say *I know* the way a person says it when they are confirming information. He says it the way a person says it when they have been sitting with a thing for several hours and are waiting for the conversation to catch up.

J waits.

"The closest thing to an omnipotent being I've ever encountered," F says. "Either of them. All three of them. The reading is what it is. Cassian's notation is accurate." A pause. "I've been in this work for thirty years. I have not hoped about it in thirty years. I am hoping about it right now."

J is quiet for long enough that the silence starts to have a shape. Then he says:

"Win."

F doesn't respond.

"I've never thought about that before. I have managed. I have contained. I have maintained. I have never once considered winning." He looks at the gray on the board. "The Leviathan is closed. Eleven years. I have looked at that marker for eleven years." Another pause. "Win."

The personal line holds for a moment. The board does not change. The gray stays gray: closed, stable, and permanently outside the category of things that require monitoring.

J ends the call. He looks at Kiyuru. Kiyuru has not moved from the window.

After a while, Kiyuru says: "There's a letter I should send."
