M0
The T429 portal moment opens into Oak City. Divineverse and Primeverse contact begins here, but this is parallel to the T-track rather than inserted between T429 and T430.

M1
Oak City's field hums low. Animals balk; dogs whine; cattle low. A hairline rip traces the air, blackening until it settles into a 35×70' harp-like gate in black/gray/orange. Heat-haze collapses. The frame stands, utterly still. Townsfolk gather and stare.

M2
An obsidian hand the size of a cart fender grips the gate's nothing-edge, then another. A titan construct pulls through, steps over the threshold, and kneels, cupping the frame with both forearms, visor down, head bowed square to it. The message is simple: don't touch.

M3
Silence thickens. Pressure arrives first—then Sera steps through: under five feet, porcelain skin, bright purple silk; thigh-high on one leg, ankle on the other; nails to match. She plants, waves, and chirps, "Hi." Everyone understands in their bones she could kill all of them.

M4
The gate flickers like fire finding focus. Ember crosses—silk echoing Sera's but stockings reversed; eyes like molten pools without pupils. She half-hesitates at the sight of people, then slides into Sera's space; Sera's arm finds her waist. The square feels permission, not threat. Her gaze catches a metal mailbox at the curb. She reaches out, curious. Her fingers close around it. The metal goes to slag in four seconds. "Oh—I'm sorry," she says, genuinely. The apology is perfectly polite. The pile of liquid metal is not.

M5
Old Mara steadies herself on her cane. Jory edges in front of children. Sheriff Cal draws breath to bark. The cantor starts a hymn on reflex; half the town hums. The sheriff's voice cracks and dies.

M6
A new pressure rolls in: ocean above, heaven underfoot. Knees loosen across the square; a few faint; fighters' bodies choose flight before thought. A scream starts somewhere; Sera raises a finger to her lips. "Shh." The sound collapses.

M7
A perfectly formed dark-tan foot with pale sole steps through, nails matching the girls. The light-lavender hem follows, then the other knee and foot. Soliera stands on the square, silent and unreadable. Everyone who can kneel, kneels. Those who can't lie prostrate. Children copy their mothers.

M8
Old Mara tries to rise; her legs won't. Sera cocks her head. "Why can't you get up?" "My legs," Mara wheezes, embarrassed. "Why are you sorry?" Sera offers both hands. "I can lift you with a finger." Mara rises—first with Sera's pull, then with her own spine.

M9
The square is still mostly knees. Sera, to Soliera: "Hmm. That's not supposed to happen. How can they love you if they're falling down?" Ember glances up, shrugs, stays tucked. A few brave chests unclench; more elders try their feet.

M10 — The First Words After the Kneeling

The silence stretched.

Soliera's eyes drifted across the square, never hurried, never sharp. Her gaze moved from banners to rooftops to cracked lines in the asphalt—then back again to the one figure who had most truly tested the moment. Old Mara. The deacon's cane trembled under her grip, her breath still uneven after the lift. But when Soliera's gaze found her, the crowd found it too. Dozens of faces turned toward Mara, as though she now bore the weight of explanation.

Sera, meanwhile, stayed fixed on the failure she had just named: pressure had put the square on its knees, and pressure-response was not love. She watched the first elders test their feet again, measuring whether worship could become chosen once bodies were free to answer.

Ember eased against Soliera's side, her emberlit hair flickering in subdued curls. She looked around with open curiosity, not menace. "Strange," she murmured, voice low but audible to those nearest. "That portal came from Undermountain, but here—" her eyes flicked toward the water towers, the asphalt, the angled shopfronts, "—this place is built in shapes I don't know. Wide blocks, long straight paths. Everything feels… flattened." She frowned faintly. "Like the world forgot to curve."

The townsfolk closest to her stared as though she were speaking prophecy. The blacksmith Jory pressed a palm over the head of the boy half-hidden behind his legs. Cantor Haim's lips moved as if he were memorizing the words for a hymn. Sheriff Cal's hand twitched at his sidearm, but no breath came with it.

Then Mara, the frail one, found her voice. She pulled her cane close, planted it firmly, and lifted her chin toward the divine figures. "If this is God," she rasped, "then we will listen. But someone must ask the question plain." She turned her head, eyes seeking the Reeve.

Tamsin stepped forward. His ledger coat still hung on his shoulders, the edges ink-stained and weather-beaten. He did not kneel—his knees had already given in earlier, and now he stood again more from stubbornness than strength. Clearing his throat, he raised his voice for all the square:

"If you're staying," he said carefully, "tell us what you need of us."

The crowd rustled. Not rebellion, not panic, but the first attempt to frame what had happened in words. Soliera gave no answer—her silence was enough to thicken the air again—but the square knew the question had been asked.

And so the town, in whispers and looks, began to turn its awe into story.


M11 — The Constructionary Conversation

Old Mara steadied herself, still flushed from being lifted. Her eyes, rheumy but sharp, lingered on Sera. She had felt it: no give at all in the girl's small frame, no bend in those porcelain arms, as if she had been hauled upright by a statue instead of flesh. Not human, her mind whispered, though her lips dared not. The kindness of the gesture and the inhumanity of the touch clashed in her chest, leaving awe and unease braided together.

Beside them, Ember tapped lightly on Soliera's hand. Soliera's eyes flicked down, then her palm shifted with quiet inevitability, pressing against the small of Ember's back. Ember leaned in closer, folding under the gesture like it was the most natural gravity in the world. Her emberlit hair curled faintly against Soliera's lavender gown as she whispered, her voice carrying just far enough to be overheard:

"Whoa… look at the roads. They're not even stone. And—there's so many people. Over there, too—look, see? More and more." Her molten gaze tracked the pale asphalt stretching through the square, the unfamiliar angles of the brick storefronts, the rows of porches crowded with gawking faces.

Sera's eyes cut toward Old Mara first, then to Jory where he still held the child behind his arm. A smirk ghosted her lips. "Don't worry," she said brightly, like she was correcting a silly mistake. "Soliera loves kids. It's strange, you hiding them away like that." Her purple-silked shoulder shrugged. "This must be a whole different area."

The words made the crowd murmur—the admission that even these beings weren't sure where they had landed, that they too were disoriented by the gate's leap. But Sera didn't sound afraid. She sounded delighted, as though the strangeness was a gift.

Jory's grip on the boy tightened; the boy peeked wide-eyed between soot-marked knuckles. Old Mara's lips parted, wanting to speak, but the words stuck. Behind them, Cantor Haim's humming faltered, trying to fold these new names into a prayer. Sheriff Cal shifted uneasily, baton creaking in his fist.

The air was still thick with Soliera's weight, but now voices and thoughts began to edge into it. The gods were not just here—they were looking, commenting, treating Oak City like a strange new country laid bare under their gaze.

And so the hush of the square tilted toward conversation, fragile but inescapable.

M12 — Hunger and the Windbreaker's Name

Sera let the silence hang long enough to savor it. Then her purple-silked shoulders rose and fell, and she glanced around at no one in particular.

"Okay," she said brightly, like she'd just remembered something obvious, "so where can we eat at?"

The question hit the square like a slap of cold water. Knees scuffed on asphalt, heads lifted, people blinked at each other. It wasn't a demand for worship, or blood, or tithe — it was the voice of a porcelain doll asking about dinner.

Ember perked up, leaning further against Soliera's steady hand. "That's a good idea," she agreed, molten eyes sweeping the faces nearest her. "There's so many people here. They must eat somewhere, right?"

A ripple of nervous laughter broke through the crowd — the absurdity of it, gods asking about supper. Soliera's silence didn't break, but the weight in the air eased as if permission had been given.

Jory shifted his stance, finally lowering the protective arm from in front of the boy. Sheriff Cal cleared his throat, but it was Old Mara who tapped her cane once and rasped, "There's the tavern, down on Elm. Closest place you'll find a plate."

At that, a younger man near the edge whispered to his neighbor, just loud enough for the murmur to carry: "Windbreaker's there. Saw her go in earlier."

The name passed through the crowd like a leaf in water, circling quick. Amara Windbreaker — the dark-skinned monk with the ember-staff, an outsider they tolerated but never claimed. Some looked uneasy at the mention; others relaxed, as if her presence made the tavern safer. Either way, the thought of her waiting there bound itself to the Triad's simple request for food.

And just like that, the next step was set: out of the square, toward the tavern, toward Amara.

M13 — Parting the Square

Soliera moved first. One unhurried step forward, and the silence deepened. The crowd parted without words, bodies tugged aside as if pulled by a tide. Bare feet brushed pale asphalt, lavender hem grazing the cracks, and people leaned back instinctively, giving her a corridor without needing to be told.

Sera gave a little hop and skip, silk flashing as she darted to Soliera's side. She looked left, right, drinking in faces and porches, eyes sharp and playful. She grinned at the sheer weight of stares—like the town itself was a stage and she had just claimed the lead role.

Ember slipped loose of Soliera's palm and moved between them, small shoulders squared, molten eyes roaming wide. Wonder bloomed across her face: shops stacked in brick, porches crowded with watchers, banners drooping under old paint. She turned her head constantly, a joy that looked half-innocent, half-reverent.

The townsfolk gave way in uneven ripples. Some crossed themselves as the women passed. Others ducked their heads, unwilling to meet those eyes again.

From the corner, Sela the runner made her choice. She glanced once at the gate, once at the goddesses, then gripped her satchel and bolted. Her slate device thumped against her hip as she sprinted, braid bouncing, shoes slapping pavement. The story would not stay in Oak City's square—by sundown, it would be racing down every road she could reach.

Behind her, a few braver hands lifted comm-slates and battered recorders. Tiny red lights winked to life as the machines captured shaky footage of the procession. A voice whispered: "If this is real, everyone has to see." Another hissed back, "Shut it off before she notices." But no one lowered their lenses.

So the square rearranged itself: the Triad moving forward, the crowd splitting into walls, whispers trailing like smoke, devices quietly recording history. Oak City was no longer just a town. It was the place where a gate had opened, a Titan had knelt, and gods now walked through its streets.

M14 — Toward the Tavern

They walked through Oak City as though the streets had been waiting for them all along. The pale asphalt bore patched seams, potholes neatly filled; porches lined with flowers and clay pots tried to soften brick and wood into welcome. Murals ran along a few walls — faded scenes of harvests, riverboats, children's games — gestures of a town that took pride in appearing whole.

Sera glanced around, smirk tugging at her lips. "Hmm. This is kinda like the Sanctum," she said, waving at the painted walls. "They put pictures everywhere too." Her tone made it sound like a child pointing out a familiar toy, but the locals nearest her lowered their heads, afraid to laugh.

Ember nodded, emberlit eyes sweeping the flowers, the murals, the porch swings creaking as people leaned back from sight. "It really is," she murmured, voice bright with sincerity. "The walls are talking here too." She fell quiet again, folding a little closer between Soliera and Sera as they walked.

Soliera said nothing. She moved steady as a tide, eyes roaming not with judgment but with that unreadable weight that made every brick, every face, every doorway feel catalogued. Wherever her gaze passed, people leaned out of it, as if afraid to be mistaken for insolence.

Ahead, the tavern came into view. It rose three stories high, brick-faced and broad-shouldered, one of the tallest buildings in town. Against the patchwork around it, it stood almost proud — matching the scale of the Divineverse halls Ember remembered. She blinked up at it, impressed. "That's… really big," she whispered, the words slipping out like awe.

Sera broke stride to skip ahead, purple silk flashing as she reached for the double doors. They were glass, framed in dark metal, handles polished by a hundred hands. She paused, staring at her own reflection there — doll-like eyes and silk hair shimmering back at her — and tilted her head, fascinated. "Huh. Mirrors for doors," she mused. "Cute."

No one in the street spoke. People walked around edges, whispering, watching from behind glass or half-open shutters. Further down the square, the sound of raised voices carried faintly — shouts of alarm and wonder as townsfolk still clustered near the Titan. It remained where it had first knelt, massive arms braced against the harp-shaped gate, head bowed in silent deference. Its presence radiated just enough to remind everyone that the miracle was still happening behind them, even as the gods reached for a tavern door.

M15 — Reflections at the Door

Sera leaned close to the glass, doll-like face tilted as she studied herself. Her reflection met her gaze with the same sharp eyes, same flawless skin, and she grinned as if she were meeting a twin across a window. She lifted her chin, smoothed her silk shoulder, and chuckled under her breath.

Ember slipped beside her, curious. She bent at the waist, watching her image stretch, then rose again, seeing it shrink. The glass warped her molten eyes, lengthened her emberlit hair into fire that seemed to lick upward. She ducked and rose a few more times, cooing with delight at the distortions. Her curls fluttered with each motion, sparks of red-gold catching against the pane.

Behind them, Soliera's figure entered the reflection. For a moment the warped glass stretched her legs absurdly, making them as long as her entire body, a lavender silhouette balanced on impossible stilts.

Sera nearly doubled over, purple silk shaking as laughter threatened to spill out of her. She slapped a hand against the glass, still giggling. "What a sight!" she managed, eyes bright. "These fancy doors are sure entertaining."

Ember, still smiling, reached toward the gleaming handles. Her fingers hovered just above the metal, studying how it caught her glow. "So we push these together?" she asked, tilting her head, her voice innocent with curiosity.

The townsfolk across the square watched in silence. To them, it was not children at play, but gods testing the threshold of Oak City's largest hall. The air outside remained taut, but at the tavern door, laughter and wonder refracted like the distorted reflections in the glass.


M16 — Inside the Tavern

Ember reached for the metal handles. She pressed first — nothing. She frowned, emberlit hair brushing her cheek, then tugged, and one side swung easily inward. Her eyes lit up. "Ahah! Easier than the doors at Thay," she said brightly, stepping back to let the others through.

Sera slipped past with a hop, purple silk fluttering as her bare feet landed squarely on the rubber welcome mat. The logo stamped across it bore both the Company's sigil and the oak-leaf emblem of the township—a subtle reminder of whose authority funded the building. She wiggled her toes against it, amused. "Oh wow. It's almost cold in here," she said, her breath catching the hum of the air conditioners overhead.

The tavern stretched wide before them, an odd hybrid of eras. Long wooden tables marched in rows across the floor, their surfaces glossy with oil and age. Behind them, shelves rose high, lined with decorative cans and glass bottles that caught the yellow-white light of modern fixtures. The bar itself was a slab of dark oak, polished smooth, but cooled by humming refrigeration units sunk behind it. A wall of mirrors reflected rows of dusty liquor bottles beside newer, plastic-labeled sodas.

The air was thick with two scents—old wood and machine chill. A fan creaked in the corner where it didn't need to, but still spun, habit stronger than efficiency.

And there, in the far corner, sat Amara Windbreaker. Her chair angled slightly away, face turned toward the wall as though she had chosen solitude deliberately. She sat at a single-person table, one elbow resting against its scarred surface, fingers tapping in idle rhythm. Her orange garments caught in the light, dulled but unmistakable; her dark hair shimmered faintly with the faint sheen of sweat or travel dust. Even seated, she gave the impression of readiness—staff leaned within arm's reach, scarf draped loose around her shoulders like it was always waiting for a gust of wind.
