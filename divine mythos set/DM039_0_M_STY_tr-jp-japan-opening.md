## TR-JP-01 — Arrival

The vehicles are waiting at the gate district.

They have been waiting there for twenty minutes: two embassy cars and a Company liaison van, double-parked at the edge of the transit hub, engines running because J ordered them running before the flight landed. The welcoming party stands on the pavement with a printed itinerary and a security protocol and the comfortable professional expectation that people will arrive at a specific exit rather than the alternative, which has not yet been operationally considered.

Kiyuru knows it before J tells him. The gate district is the wrong kind of quiet.

In the shrine courtyard, six people are standing in the morning air.

The courtyard is flagstone, older than the surrounding district by several centuries, shaded under cedar that has not been asked to accommodate the vertical geometry built above and around it. On three sides of the shrine compound, the city rises — glass, steel, signage in two alphabets, the structural grammar of a megacity managing forty million people's vertical ambitions one floor at a time. The fourth side is open sky, and on the horizon, visible above the residential tier, a mech frame stands at standby on its maintenance platform like a ship in dry dock: enormous, articulated, patient. Not threatening. Just very large and present in the way of things built to be large and present so that other things are not.

The shrine does not acknowledge any of this. The shrine has been here longer and is not concerned.

Sera is looking at it.

"Oh," she says. "This is pretty."

She says it the way she says most things she means — plainly, without ceremony, which in this context produces a small silence from Kiyuru, who had organized an itinerary, and from J, who had briefed against an itinerary, and from Letha, who is looking in the same direction Sera is looking and also finds it pretty. The cedar canopy holds the morning light in slats. The shrine's main building is a material statement about permanence — not large, not imposing, but visibly uninterested in the city's opinions about it.

The kami presence registers underneath the silence like a held breath, or a change in air pressure, or the specific feeling of a space that has been treated with sustained reverence long enough that it has begun to take the treatment seriously. Kiyuru reads it hardest. He is a man whose professional framework sits at the intersection of institutional strategy and sacred artifact custody, which means he has spent his adult life categorizing things by their position in one system or the other. What arrived in this courtyard twenty seconds ago does not have a position. What arrived has a weight he can feel through the flagstone and an ambient quality that the kami presence is responding to the way a second instrument responds to a tuning fork — not in submission, but in recognition. He files this. He does not know yet what file it belongs in.

J is already sending a message to the gate district. *Abort transport. We are on site.* He reads the response — three question marks from the liaison van — and sends a second message that is more definitive.

"I can translate," Kiyuru says. He means this practically.

Sera looks at him. The morning light is doing something interesting with the cedar, and she has the relaxed expression of someone whose morning is going well.

"I appreciate that," she says. "I'm also —" She tips her head, searching, and lands on: "Niv calls me an omelet. It means I can do everything all the time."

Kiyuru files this. The file remains open.

J, beside him, is reading the translation in the tablet's language assist function and has the brief expression of a man deciding whether the context that would explain that sentence is available to him. He determines it is not. He closes the app.

The shrine holds its breath. The cedar canopy moves once in a light off the mountain. The mech on the horizon has not changed position.


## TR-JP-02 — The Family

Hisako comes through the side entrance first.

She sees J before she sees the others, which is an ordering of priorities rather than a failure of perception — J has been to the shrine before, which means the courtyard has learned to expect him as the leading edge of whatever arrives next. She offers him the abbreviated greeting of someone already thinking past the greeting, and then she looks at the courtyard and feels the rest of it at the same time.

She does not reach for a framework. She reaches for composure, which is the response of a woman who has lived in proximity to sacred objects long enough to know that the correct behavior in an unexpected threshold moment is to be calm and hospitable and to evaluate later.

"Welcome," she says, in Japanese.

Sera smiles at her. Ember waves.


Tsubaki is not planning to come out.

She had been in the residence, in the particular orientation of someone doing three things with the focused incuriosity of a person who has decided not to be curious about the thing happening in the courtyard, and then the something in the air changes — or she runs out of incuriosity — and she comes out through the back door in short shorts and a sports bra, the kind of outfit that represents a decision about where she is and what the courtyard is allowed to require of her. She is a hair taller than Sera.

She does not fold. She meets the eye contact and holds it with the quality of someone who has decided, on approximately one second of information, that folding would be the wrong call.

Sera likes her immediately. This is visible.

The nuzzle happens before it is a decision — Sera close, Sera's cheek against the side of Tsubaki's head in the brief affectionate press of someone whose body operates on instinct before courtesy catches up.

"I think you're gonna be a great addition to the team," Sera says.

Tsubaki does not say anything. Her hand goes to Sera's back. Keeps it there.


Ember has been looking at a lantern mounted on the shrine's outer wall. She is not reading the inscription. She is looking at it the way she looks at objects that carry a history she does not have the language for yet. Then she looks at Kiyuru.

"Your dad isn't here," she says. Not an accusation. An observation.

Kiyuru is very still for a moment. "No."

Ember considers this. "Is he in a place where he could come back?"

Letha is standing close enough that she catches the question before it lands on the gathering's formal register. She says, carefully and not loudly: "That's something that might be possible. Not a guarantee. But it's something that might be on the table."

Hisako has heard. She looks at J. J looks at neither of them in particular, which is the face he makes when a conversation has moved into territory he is professionally obligated to track but personally obligated to let the family own.

The Japanese-language conversation that follows is not long, but it is three-sided and carries the specific weight of a discussion where every participant agrees on the stakes and disagrees on the correct moral posture. Kiyuru has one position. Hisako has another. J does not take a position, which is itself a position.

Sera has been watching. She is standing with Tsubaki, who has not moved away.

"Y'all relax," Sera says. "You're spending too much time thinking."

She takes Tsubaki's hand and starts up the path toward the residence entrance.

Tsubaki goes. She looks back at the deliberation once, briefly.

"Onii-chan," she says — to Kiyuru, over her shoulder — "why wouldn't we do it."

This is not a question. Kiyuru looks at the path. Then at his mother. Then at the courtyard, which has been part of his family's history for eleven generations and has its own weight of things that were and were not reversible. Hisako has the expression of a woman whose worry has been redistributed to a smaller area than expected — softer than it was an hour ago, softer than it should have been.

The decision is not made. It is also not not-made.


## TR-JP-03 — Entry to the House

The genkan holds six pairs of shoes and one pair of bare feet.

Sera does not dirty her feet. This is not a house rule she was told; it is a property of her, the way most properties of Sera are — present without explanation, consistent without negotiation. The toenails are painted plum. Soliera is wearing a yukata. The color matches. This is probably not a coincidence but it is also not a conversation anyone has out loud.

Hisako takes them through the entrance, through the room that manages the transition between the outside and the house's interior logic, through the corridor where the residence stops being architecture and becomes space that has been lived in long enough to have character. She is a competent guide in the way of someone who has calibrated this tour for guests who require it. The girls do not require it. They walk through it anyway, which Hisako registers as courtesy.

The central room is low-ceilinged, floored in tatami, arranged around a table built for a family. Three cushions are set at it. Kiyuru had prepared for less than this.


Ember asks about food approximately forty seconds after sitting down, which is her customary interval between arrival and the question of whether food is available.

"Is there something to eat?" she asks. She is not demanding. She is asking, which is a real distinction in Ember's grammar.

Hisako straightens. The hospitality stress activates: she has not prepared for this, not in the way of the question specifically, not for guests of this particular category in this number on this timeline. She begins an assessment of the kitchen.

Ember looks at Soliera. "Could you make some food for us?"

The table already has food on it.

Not in the process of being covered — already covered, which is a distinction that takes a moment to land. A full setting: Japanese dishes alongside things that are not Japanese, a platter of fruit, a configuration that includes at minimum one item that has no business being in a Japanese kitchen and is nonetheless present, plated, warm. Soliera has not visibly moved. Hisako is looking at the table with the expression of a woman updating several frameworks simultaneously.

Sera has already taken a piece of dragonfruit from the far platter. She looks at the cushion situation: three, which is correct for the family and mathematically incorrect for the current attendance.

"It's okay," she says, waving off Kiyuru before he moves toward the storage room. She drifts to the couch in the adjoining room and sits sideways on it the way she sits sideways on most things, dragonfruit in hand, entirely comfortable in a room she has been in for ninety seconds.


Hisako excuses herself before the meal is finished. She says she needs to rest, which is true and also the kind of truth that is doing additional work — there is a particular quality to her exit, the quality of someone going somewhere to think, or to be horizontal, or to pray. The room where she goes is off the main corridor, and the sounds through the structure's walls are what a house transmits and a family learns to read: the specific silence that is occupied rather than empty, the faint cadence of someone addressing something they do not have another address for.

Kiyuru does not pretend not to hear it. He is his family's eldest and the custodian of an eleven-generation inheritance and he knows what his mother sounds like when she is asking for guidance. The worry, underneath the sound of it, is softer than it should have been. Softer than it was before the courtyard. Something in the afternoon has redistributed it.

He is not certain yet what that means. He files it with the other things he is not certain about yet.

Outside, across the transit tier and the residential stacks, the mech on the skyline has not moved.
