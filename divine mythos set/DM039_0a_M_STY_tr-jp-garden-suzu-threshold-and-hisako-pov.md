## TR-JP-04 — The Garden

The meal does not end so much as redistribute.

Tsubaki had come in from the side corridor intending to retrieve a glass of water and had instead ended up at the table corner with a cushion and a small plate of rice, which is the kind of outcome the Tachibana table tends to produce in people who pass near it when it is occupied and active. She does not seem to find this surprising. She takes her place at the corner and occupies it with the ease of someone who has sat at this table a thousand times and does not require it to be different because of who else is at it. Ember looks at her with the frank appreciative eye of someone who has found a new interesting person and is deciding which question to ask first.

She asks Tsubaki what she likes to do. This is where Ember begins with most people: not who they are, but what they do with their time.

Hisako returns from the prayer room while this is happening. She comes through the corridor with the particular quiet of someone who has been in a room that carries its own weight, and she reads the table the way she reads most things in her house — quickly, by the grammar of who is where and what their posture means. The corner cushion is now occupied. The food is still substantial. She registers that Sera is not at the table, and then she does not have to look very hard, because she is already looking at the garden door.


The garden door requires knowing to push rather than pull. Sera figures this out in the third second she tries it and goes through.

The path is six stones from the door to the plum tree. The stones are moss-worn and settled by decades of patient compression — they accept the foot without complaint. Sera walks them in bare feet with the unconsidered ease of someone who has never had reason to worry about what the ground is doing. The plum tree at the end of the path is old and has been managing its own shape longer than anyone in the compound has been alive. It has dropped two small unripe fruits near the root base. They are there.

She looks at it for a moment.

The paper bag Hisako had given her at the table — mochi, four flavors, the top folded twice with the careful precision of a host for whom presentation is itself a form of welcome — she is holding this by the fold. She looks at the tree. Then she folds the paper bag into quarters and doesn't leave.

She sits instead, on the flat stone at the base of the roots, with the decisiveness of someone who has evaluated the furniture of a situation and found it sufficient. Bare feet flat on the moss beside the stone. The paper bag in her lap, folded small. Somewhere in the garden's north end, a cricket is doing what crickets do, unimpressed by the day's theological weight.

Tsubaki comes through the door approximately five seconds behind her.

She does not ask if there's room on the stone. There is room, and she sits, and the space accepts both of them with the equanimity of a garden that has held people for a long time.

"Your yard," Tsubaki says.

Not a challenge. A fact: you are a guest in this space, not a sovereign of it.

Sera considers this. It lands clean — the specific comfort of being given the shape of a room and finding it correct.

"Yeah," she says. She looks at the plum tree. "Good yard."

The cricket continues. The plum tree does not move. The garden holds the afternoon light through the cedar on the north wall with the unhurried competence of a garden that has been doing this for a long time. Tsubaki does not say anything else. Sera does not move. The paper bag, folded to quarters, sits in her lap.


## TR-JP-05 — Cold Open (Suzu Hikari)

The titan is in standby on its outdoor platform — plum-and-white conviction arranged into the geometry of patience, twelve stories of it, because that is the operational altitude for a thing built from the accumulated belief of a country that needed something between itself and the rest of the weight class, and built it anyway.

There are seventeen people in the immediate radius doing things that are not about the titan. Three ministry observers with documentation protocols. Two engineers running a maintenance diagnostic on the right shoulder assembly — a scuff from the last public demonstration that Suzu flagged for resurfacing, because it was bothering her in the specific and complete way that small imperfections on the titan always bother her. A scheduling coordinator working through a tablet with seventeen items and no gaps, because Japan does not leave gaps in the calendar of its primary strategic asset.

Suzu is sitting on the edge of the platform with her feet hanging over a sixty-foot drop, eating a rice ball.

The bag is beside her. The model is in the bag. These facts are not separable.

She is thirty years old and she has been at the center of this radius since she was nineteen, and she has made her peace with it the way you make peace with something that is also genuinely yours — not cleanly, not all at once, but durably. The titan is hers. The ministry budget is a consequence of that, not a condition of it. What she has never made peace with is the specific shape of the loneliness that the apparatus creates: she has been wanted plenty and approached essentially never, because anyone approaching her also approaches the robot, the national schedule, the security perimeter, the public myth, and eleven years of accumulated institutional weight. The price of admission, as a number, is not zero.

The air changes.

She doesn't reach for a category for it. She doesn't need one. In eleven years of operating at this level she has learned what it feels like when something of comparable weight enters the same general register — not as a threat signal, not as a challenge, as a fact — and the air is doing that now. The titan in standby does not move, but if it were a tuning fork it would be ringing. She sets the rice ball down.

She looks toward the shrine district without deciding to look in that direction.

*That's interesting.*


She sees them first at distance: two figures in what she can read as a garden attached to the Tachibana compound. Not close enough for detail. Close enough that the texture of the air between her position and theirs is not the same texture it was an hour ago.

Her professional register runs underneath everything, always: *weight class, source type, threat-or-not.* The assessment comes back in four seconds and the answer is not alarming. The answer is the kind of not-alarming that is its own specific category of interesting — the one that means whoever is sitting in that garden has a baseline that would give even the titan something to work with, and they are sitting in a garden eating something off a paper bag.

She looks toward the scheduling coordinator without turning all the way.

"Clear the platform for an hour."

"The diagnostic is mid-sequence—"

"Leave the engineers. Clear the observers."

He marks something on his tablet and moves. She is practiced at these small negotiations; she is practiced at getting what she asks for, because after eleven years the apparatus has learned when she is being inconvenient and when she is being specific, and this is the second one. The observers clear. The coordinator finds somewhere else to document. The engineers continue on the shoulder assembly.

Suzu looks back toward the garden.

Neither of the figures has looked in her direction. Or if they have, they have not shown it. She finishes the rice ball. She checks the bag by weight — model present, model intact. Good.

The platform is quiet now except for the engineers and the ambient sound of the city doing its midday work sixty feet below. Suzu sits alone on the edge and does what she does with things she does not have immediate categories for: she pays attention to them. She does not default to cautious when she does not have to. She is not a cautious person; she is a careful one, which is a different instrument with a different use.

What is over there is something she would like to know more about. She files this and stays where she is, and the titan holds its standby position above her in the afternoon light, and the air between the platform and the shrine district continues to carry whatever it is carrying.

## TR-JP — Hisako: The Morning Before (Parallel POV, Pre-Dawn Through Arrival)

*Parallel account covering the Tachibana household's interiority from Hisako's pre-dawn shrine rounds through the courtyard arrival. The corresponding on-page account begins at TR-JP-01.*


Hisako knows each stone.

This is not a figure of speech. She has been making the pre-dawn rounds since her husband taught her the order — south path first, then the offering shelf, then the cedar walk, then back through the side gate — and the stones in the dark are as familiar as the interior of her own hand. The lantern she carries is a formality now, or a courtesy, or a habit from the years when it was necessary; she has not been able to determine which of those three things to assign it, and so she carries it.

The offering shelf receives its morning rice. She does this without the particular consciousness of ritual — less performing a thing than continuing it, the way breathing continues. The cedar walk is cold for June. The moss along the gate's interior base is still wet from overnight mist, and she makes a note of this the way she always makes a note of it, which is to know and not to think about.

Chiyo is on the step.

She is sitting upright and present in a way that Hisako reads as awake rather than transitioned — ninety-two years old and apparently done consulting her body about its schedule, which has been the case for the better part of a month. Hisako sets the lantern down.

"Go back inside. It's cold."

"Something's coming," Chiyo says.

She says it the way she says things about the trees — as information received from a source she has never been asked to explain and has never tried to. She has been saying things about the trees for thirty years. She has been right often enough that Hisako stopped asking her to qualify.

"The visitors," Hisako says. "Kiyuru arranged it. They're coming this afternoon."

Chiyo is quiet in the specific way that means she is not disagreeing. "Something else," she says. "Already on the way."

Hisako helps her back inside. The sky is doing its pre-dawn work, the horizon shifting from black toward the blue that does not yet announce itself as morning. She stands at the gate for a moment after Chiyo is settled, looking at the courtyard. The courtyard looks the way it has looked every morning for eleven years.

She goes in. She tries to sleep. She does not sleep.


Tsubaki is on the stairs.

Not on her way down — sitting on the fourth step from the bottom in the posture of someone who woke up and found themselves already awake and decided not to fight it. Sleep clothes. Eyes alert in a way that teenagers do not manage before seven in the morning; Tsubaki has it at five.

"I couldn't sleep," she says.

"The visitors."

"Yeah." A pause. "And something else. I don't know."

Hisako looks at her daughter for a moment — this daughter who has been Kiyuru's *onii-chan* from the time she could say it, who has a quality of not-folding in the face of difficult things that sometimes reads as carelessness and is not. "Try to sleep if you can," she says.

"I'll try."

She tries. By six she is up for good. By seven she is in the kitchen, then briefly outside, then back in looking like someone who has confirmed something they had already guessed.

Hisako does not ask what. She is beginning to understand that the day is going to answer this on its own.


She reads Kiyuru's briefing for the third time over morning tea.

He had said it quietly: *there are beings now that changed everything we understood about power. Not government. Not military. Not what we had.* He had said it and then said it again more quietly, which is how she knows when Kiyuru is not exaggerating. She has prayed about this morning more than she prayed about any morning in years — not to be reassured, but to locate her own feet before the ground changed. She wants to be calm and hospitable and to do her family credit. She wants to be the kind of woman whose composure does not require the situation to be smaller than it is.

She is in the garden when it happens.

She is checking the north path stones — one of them has been settling at an angle and she is assessing whether it needs resetting before the afternoon, which is the kind of attention she gives things she can control when she is anticipating things she cannot — and the morning is ordinary and quiet and then it is not.

There is no sound. Or not much sound. What there is instead is a displacement of air that is not wind, and then six people are standing in the courtyard in the way that people do not arrive in courtyards unless they came through the gate, and the gate is twenty feet to her left and has not opened.

Hisako looks at them.

The tall one at the center: the quality of how she occupies the flagstone is not what a person does with a space, or it is what a person does, but not only that. The one beside her — Hisako does not have a word for the sense she gets, which is of intelligence that has assembled itself into the form it wears and is still taking in what forms are — she is looking at the cedar canopy with patient attention. And the small one, barefoot, is looking at the shrine's main structure with the kind of appreciation that does not perform itself — direct, sincere, the look of something that recognizes old effort and has an opinion about it.

The courtyard's ambient register is doing something Hisako has never felt it do. She has maintained this space for eleven years and she knows its character the way she knows the stones on the south path. What it is doing now is not rejection. It is not alarm. It is acknowledgment — the specific response of something that has been treated with sustained reverence and is now in the presence of what the reverence was pointing toward. She processes this in the background, because J is coming through the gate and his face is familiar and reading the situation and not alarmed, and she extracts from this what she can.

Then the small one says something quietly, looking at the shrine.

Hisako cannot hear the words from across the courtyard. She reads the register instead: appreciation. Genuine. Not performed.

She thinks: *oh.*

She thinks: *I did not actually understand what he meant.*

She thinks: *I thought I understood what he meant.*

She reaches for composure. It is there. It has always been there when she has reached for it, and she has been reaching for it her whole adult life.

She crosses the courtyard. "Welcome," she says. She means it entirely.
