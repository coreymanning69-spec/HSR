## BADLANDS TO BREACH — PART 2: WALL BREACH, RISA, AND THE ALLEYWAYS

The floodlights twitched. Converged.

What had been sweeping arcs narrowed — three beams locking down onto one small figure in the dust. Ember, hair flaring under the glare, obsidian skin turned slick and reflective.


M111 — SERA STEPS IN FRONT

Before the cameras could fully re-center, Sera moved. One blink she was at Soliera's side, hands behind her head. The next, the feeds jittered — two frames of static, a smear of blue silk — and she was in front of Ember, facing the wall. Between Ember and the guns. Empty hands raised in a lazy, almost mocking wave.

Soliera kept walking, unbothered, Her pace unbroken.

"Eww," Ember muttered again, quieter, more annoyed than scared. "Why are they pointing it at me?"


M112 — TOWER THREE / INTENTION DECIDED

Inside the North Gate tower, a gunner flinched as his targeting overlay went red, glitched, and caught up. For a moment he'd had a clean lock on a small moving heat source. Then the picture steadied and there were two targets in the same spot, one partially occluding the other.

"Command, this is Tower Three," he said into his mic, voice thinning. "I've got — I've got something. Two female-presenting unknowns approaching the wall on foot. No visible armor. Not on the road. They're... waving?"

The comms crackled — other towers checking in, someone in the hub swearing, a tech rattling off distance numbers that didn't make sense. Then a voice broke through, clipped and urgent: the cease order, transmitted from Executive F's channel through the chain above the tower, arriving at the gunner's ear.

"Do not fire. Repeat, do NOT—"

The girl on the screen looked straight into the camera. She smiled like she knew exactly where he was sitting.

UNKNOWN ENTITY [NO ARMOR]
THREAT LEVEL: EVALUATING…

His training wasn't subtle. Unknowns approaching off-route, ignoring lights, closing on the wall. His thumb found the safety out of habit, rubbing the ridged plastic. The cease order was in his ear. He knew it was there.

The girl wiggled her fingers in a little wave.

His thumb flicked the safety off.

He brought the rifle up a fraction — just enough for the sights to climb her sternum, the targeting reticle tightening from a loose white circle to a sharp, pulsing red dot.

Outside, at the base of the wall, Sera was still smiling up into the light.

She hadn't moved yet.

The intention had been decided. That was enough.


M114–M115: THE BREACH / THE RETCON

Every turret along that section snapped red at once.

There was no beam. No visible arc. Just a sound like the sky folding in on itself — and then absence.

The entire gunline above them sheared off.

From the city's side it was like watching a zipper rip open across the crown of the wall: emplacements, walkways, parapet, all the careful teeth of concrete and alloy gone in a horizontal slice. The top thirty feet of fortification and a seventy-foot stretch directly in front of Ember simply weren't there anymore.

The shockwave rolled into the city like a giant's open hand. Windows blew inward for block after block — glass coughing out into apartments, offices, storefronts. Alarms screamed. Cars skidded, horns locked. On the outer face, tons of wall and steel and gun tore free in ugly chunks and came down like jagged rain. Concrete slabs cratered the badlands. Twisted barrels punched through the roofs of vehicles that had been too close.

Dozens of people died before they understood what had gone wrong.

At ground level, in front of the new gap, the girls barely moved. Dust surged out toward them and broke around something that wasn't wind, curling away from Soliera's quiet presence and Sera's solid frame.

Sera clapped twice — sharp, happy little sounds completely at odds with the devastation. She planted one hand on her hip, chin up, blue silk fluttering in the hot gusts from the collapsing wall.

"Ah, there we go," she said brightly. "Did you know they tried to shoot at us?"

Amara realized only then that she'd stopped breathing.

Her lungs burned as she dragged air back in. She'd seen Sera cut men in half, erase wizards, turn battlefields into art — but this was an entire fortification gone because one person, in one tower, had let a targeting reticle touch Sera's chest. The cease order had been sent. It had arrived. It hadn't mattered. The intent was already in the hands before the order reached the ears.

Ember blinked up at the ruined gun housings, tilting her head.

"What are the guns? They're toys, like you said before?"

Sera looked back at her, and the grin softened into something almost gentle. "Toys to us. But they use them to end other beings' lives."

Soliera stepped up beside them. She stood there, content, eyes on the open wound in the wall and the chaos beyond — as if this was exactly where They were always meant to enter.

Amara stood and looked at the gap. The smoke. The sirens already starting to rise.

A tear slipped down her cheek before she even noticed.

Soliera turned Her head. Not all the way — just enough that Amara knew She'd seen.

Sera caught it too. "Amara," she asked, direct as always, "they were going to kill you. You are upset?"

Not accusing. Literal. Input, output. They aimed. They died. You're safe. Why the tear?

Amara opened her mouth and nothing coherent came out. They were going to shoot. She didn't want to die. She also didn't want this — this level of consequence — to be how the city first met Them. Not like this. She shut her mouth again. The best she could manage was a small, miserable shake of the head that meant yes and no at the same time.

Ember looked at Amara, saw the tear, then turned to Soliera with a sudden bright grin as if the solution were obvious.

"Soli," she said, tugging lightly at Soliera's sleeve. "Can you fix it?"


M115 — THE CORRECTION

Soliera could, of course.

The air around Her was already listening.

She raised Her hand — not a dramatic gesture, nothing that would photograph well or make a story, just a motion like reaching for something just out of frame — and the correction settled over the city like a second breath.

People who had been dying were not dying. People who had been dead were standing again, in the positions they'd last occupied, with glass still sharp in the window frames around them and the echo of the shockwave still present in their ears and in the memories of the people they'd been talking to. A woman whose husband had been on the phone with her from inside the wall would later describe hearing everything — the sound, the silence, and then his voice again — and not knowing, for the rest of her life, how to explain it.

The gap in the wall remained. That was not undone. The guns, the battlements, the thirty feet of fortification — those stayed gone. Soliera fixed the living things. The architecture stayed honest.

Amara stood very still for a moment.

"They're..." she started, watching the distant figures through the gap moving, upright, bewildered. "They were just — and now they're..."

"Yes," Ember said, matter-of-factly, watching the same thing. "Soli does that."

Amara looked at Ember. Ember looked back at her with the calm of someone who has seen this enough times to have filed it correctly.

"They remember," Amara said.

"Yes," Ember said again.

Amara breathed in. Breathed out. Somewhere behind the gap in the wall, people who had been dead were now calling each other on phones and were going to spend the rest of their lives trying to find the words for what they remembered. The city was already in motion — people moving toward the gap, toward the open air, toward the four figures standing at its threshold. Not attacking. Not fleeing. Moved by the particular animal pull of something unprecedented, the way crowds always move toward the thing they cannot explain.

The girls walked toward the gap.


M116–M121: FIRST CONTACT / RISA

The SUVs arrived before the crowd did.

Two vehicles, Company markings, moving fast and then stopping hard. Doors opened. People poured out — corporate security, analysts, two uniformed officials, and then, from the second vehicle, one small half-elf woman who was very clearly there because someone had needed a body and she'd been the nearest one who wasn't fully human.

Her name was Risa. She was a low-tier assistant, a translator and administrative resource who had been pulled into this morning's chain of communication because someone in the chain above her had decided that whatever these arrivals were, they'd respond better to someone who wasn't entirely corporate, entirely human, entirely anything. She had been given almost no briefing. She had been given the title "interpreter" in the same tone someone uses when they say "volunteer."

She was terrified.

She walked toward the gap in the wall with her hands steady and her pulse at approximately twice its resting rate, and she was doing the specific calculation that only people of certain dispositions do in survival situations: being noticed, from this far up the chain, was itself a form of honor. She was inside an event that would be written about. Her name would be in the record somewhere. That was worth the fear. Probably.

The wall event, she had already decided, was mass hysteria. Something had happened — a structural failure, a weapons discharge, an anomaly — and the stress of it had produced a shared perceptual event. This was the most rational explanation. She was holding it firmly.

She looked at the four figures standing at the gap's edge.

The rational explanation developed several structural cracks.

The girl in blue silk was looking back at her with an expression of uncomplicated curiosity, the way someone looks at a menu they haven't decided about yet. Beside her, a small obsidian-skinned figure was examining a piece of rubble she'd picked up, turning it over in her hands with the focused attention of a researcher at a dig site. And at the center, standing in the light that spilled through the gap — something Risa's hindbrain did not have a category for and tried to file under several different headings before giving up entirely and simply producing the response: do not look away.

The officials behind her were saying things. Names, titles, official language about jurisdiction and incident protocols and the necessity of a formal venue.

Sera smiled. "Hi! I'm Sera." She pointed. "That's Ember." She pointed again. "And this is God."

Complete ease. No performance. Just: that's who we are, hello.

An official to Risa's left opened his mouth and nothing came out of it for several seconds.

Then Soliera walked toward the gate, and the gate — the inner gate, the formal processing gate that had been closed and sealed since the incident — opened. Not because anyone operated it. Because She was walking toward it, and things in Her path tended to reach conclusions about what was most appropriate and act accordingly.

They walked into the city.

People stopped panicking. Not because anything had been said or resolved — but because humans habituate fast, and the operational logic of the crowd had shifted from "something terrible is happening" to "if we weren't dead yet, maybe don't run." The city was in a panic-to-paralysis cycle, and the paralysis had arrived.

Risa walked beside Sera on the main drag — or tried to; the specific geometry of walking next to these four kept requiring small corrections she couldn't account for, as if the space around them operated under slightly different rules and she was the only one who had to negotiate them.


M119 — RISA RAISES HER HAND

She raised her hand.

Amara, keeping pace nearby, caught it with a quiet sideways look and leaned in.

"That doesn't work," she said, under her breath. "You have to call out."

Risa lowered her hand. Cleared her throat.

"If your God—"

Amara's elbow found her ribs. Not hard. Precise.

"Since," Risa said, recalibrating mid-sentence. "Since your God is here… would you walk with me to my home?"


M120 — QUEEN SERA NOW

Sera's head turned. The full, unhurried attention of someone who had been keeping most of her awareness on the city and had just reallocated it to this specific question.

"Okay!" she said. Then, as an afterthought that was not an afterthought: "But it's Queen Sera now. I used to be a lady, now I have a kingdom."

Behind them, Ember made a sound of pure approval and clapped twice — fast, small, the kind of clapping that is not applause but confirmation, the physical expression of: yes, this is correct, I am pleased.

Amara turned her face toward the nearest storefront and found it very interesting.

"Of course," Risa said, after a beat. "My apologies, Queen Sera."

"No, no." Sera had already turned back to the street. "You didn't know. Now you know."

People on the main drag stopped. This was not the organized stopping of a crowd that has been warned or corralled — this was the spontaneous, involuntary stillness of people who encounter something at close range that does not match any stored file and requires a full system pause to process. Traffic slowed. Someone dropped their coffee.

Behind her, Risa could hear the officials trying to establish a route — to the corporate welcome facility, to the formal reception space, to anything with a controlled environment and a conference table. She could hear the plan, and she could see, from where she was walking, exactly where the plan was going to break down.

She'd grown up in the slums.

The northeast quarter. Grid 7, Alley 14, three floors up in a building that had survived on stubbornness and community and not much else. She knew those streets the way she knew her own hands. And she had learned, in the two minutes since walking through that gate, enough about these four people to know with certainty that any official venue would be the wrong venue.


M121 — RISA TURNS LEFT

She made the call.

At the corner of the main drag and the northeast feeder, Risa turned left.

The officials behind her did not turn left. There was a beat of confusion, a beat of disbelief, and then the beginning of the kind of protest that uses words like "protocol" and "authorized route" and "this is extremely irregular."

Sera turned left without hesitation, because Risa had turned left and left looked more interesting than the straight corporate corridor. Ember turned left immediately and was already halfway down the feeder before anyone said anything. Soliera turned left with the unhurried clarity of something that has already been going this direction and had simply been allowing the map to catch up.

Amara turned left. She looked back at the officials. She shrugged with the expression of a woman who has given up on controlled environments and has found a kind of peace in that.

The officials did not turn left.

In Oak City command, the comms line to Executive F lit up. Senior corporate personnel in Neo-Moscow were communicating their deep concerns about the unauthorized deviation from the approved route and the actions of an administrative resource named Risa.

Niv listened. He let them finish.

Then he said: "Sera has claimed her."

There was a pause on the other end of the line. The kind of pause that means: I do not know what that means but I understand that it matters.

"She is," Niv continued, with the patience of someone explaining a geological fact to someone who has just noticed the mountain for the first time, "functionally the head of state of the Sanctum. You will not pursue disciplinary action. You will not reassign her. You will not restrict her access. She is untouchable."

He did not explain further. He ended the call and turned back to his screens.

Executive F, who had been in the room for this, wrote down: Risa — Sera-claimed / untouchable. Then he filed it under things I will need later and poured himself a second cup of coffee.

Outside the command post, through the window that looked out over the Oak City plaza, the city was doing what it had been doing since the girls arrived: quietly becoming better than it had been. Flower growth had appeared in the median strips overnight — nobody had planted anything. Businesses near the plaza were running the best weeks of their operational lives without being able to articulate why. Three separate Company health reports filed in the last two days showed measurably improved baseline readings in residents who had simply been near the Sanctum presence.

Cassian stood at the window and looked at it. Veyren stood beside him, arms folded, looking at the same thing.

"It's the ambient effect," Cassian said.

"Yes," Veyren said.

"They didn't do anything specific."

"No."

"They just... were here."

Veyren looked at him. "Yes," he said again.

Cassian turned back to the screens where Niv was already cataloging the slum feeds — not just watching but analyzing: socio-political data, infrastructure metrics, population behavioral patterns, theological implications. He had been doing this since they arrived. He had learned every piece of modern technology in the command post within a week of stepping through the Gate and had immediately started using all of it simultaneously.

"Does he sleep?" Cassian asked.

"I haven't seen it," Veyren said.
